Late delivery costs 1.73 stars.

A Power BI model built on 100,000 Brazilian marketplace orders. The brief was "where is the revenue coming from?" — the model answered a more useful question: what quietly destroys customer satisfaction, and how much of the catalogue actually matters.

WHAT THE DATA SAID

Four findings that
changed the story.

01

Delivery time predicts satisfaction better than anything else

Orders delivered on time average 4.29 stars. Orders that miss the promised date average 2.57. That 1.73-star gap is wider than the spread between any two product categories — so the fastest route to a better review score is logistics, not merchandising.

Past 30 days in transit, the average review falls to 2.18 stars (4,032 orders)
02

A quarter of the catalogue earns 80% of the revenue

17 of 71 categories generate 80% of sales. Health & beauty (9.4%), watches & gifts (9.0%) and bed/bath/table (7.7%) lead. A Pareto cumulative measure on the Product Performance page makes that cut-off visible rather than assumed, so range decisions have a line to point at.

03

Revenue is geographically concentrated

São Paulo alone is 38.3% of revenue; add Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais and three of 27 states account for 63.4%. Growth and delivery performance therefore have to be read per state — a national average hides both the opportunity and the risk.

04

Almost nobody comes back

Only 3.12% of 96,096 customers ever place a second order. This is invisible unless the model counts people correctly — and it is the finding most likely to be reported wrongly, for the reason in the next section.

THE PART THAT TAKES THE TIME

Three modelling traps
in this dataset.

The analysis is only trustworthy because of what happens before the first chart. Each of these silently produces a plausible-looking wrong answer.

Counting customers

customer_id is unique per order, not per person. Counting it makes every customer look new and repeat-purchase analysis meaningless. The model counts customer_unique_id instead — which is how the 3.12% repeat rate surfaced.

The geolocation table

geolocation.csv holds 1,000,163 rows, many per zip prefix. Related as-is it creates a many-to-many join that quietly inflates every aggregate. It is de-duplicated to one row per geolocation_zip_code_prefix before it becomes a lookup.

Encoding

order_reviews.csv is Portuguese. Under the default encoding the accents mojibake and category and comment text become unusable. Every query loads with Encoding = 65001 (UTF-8).

HOW IT IS BUILT

Model and measures.

A star schema with order_items as the fact table, and orders, customers, products, sellers and reviews as dimensions. All shaping happens in Power Query so the raw extract stays untouched and reproducible.

BEING STRAIGHT ABOUT IT

Scope and honesty.

What this is, and what it isn't

This is a self-directed project on the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist (Olist & André Sionek, published on Kaggle under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). It is not client work, and no business acted on it — so there is no "we saved X" claim attached to it.

What it does show is the work itself: choosing a grain, catching the joins that would have produced confident nonsense, writing measures that answer a decision rather than fill a canvas, and landing on a finding specific enough to argue with.

Known limits — 2016 is sparse, so trends are read from Jan 2017; item price and payment value differ slightly because of vouchers and instalment rounding; and delivery metrics only include orders with a recorded delivery date.
